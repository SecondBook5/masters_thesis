\chapter{Introduction} \label{chap:intro}

\section{Premalignancy is a tissue-level process}
\label{sec:intro-tissue}

Cancer does not begin at the moment of invasion. It is preceded by a sequence of premalignant
lesions in which epithelial cells acquire progression-associated regulatory and genomic changes while
still confined to their tissue of origin (premalignancy-review). In the lung, this sequence runs from
histologically normal epithelium through atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) and adenocarcinoma in
situ (AIS) to minimally invasive and frankly invasive adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (luad-precursor-path).
These lesions are not merely smaller tumors; they are qualitatively distinct tissue states, and the
central clinical problem they pose is that only a fraction progress, while current histology cannot
reliably distinguish those that will (precursor-risk). A mechanistic account of what pushes a
premalignant epithelial cell toward invasion---and what can be read from a single biopsy---would
change how these lesions are managed.

A recurring theme in modern cancer biology is that this push is not cell-intrinsic alone. Progression
is shaped by the tissue ecosystem the epithelial cell inhabits: the inflammatory, stromal, and
vascular compartments that surround it and remodel across the precursor sequence (tme-review). This
motivates a host-versus-intrinsic framing that runs throughout this dissertation---an epithelial cell's
fate reflects both its own regulatory state and the physiologic state of the tissue around it, and
separating these two contributions is a prerequisite for understanding, and eventually predicting,
progression.

\section{Lung and pancreatic precursor systems}
\label{sec:intro-systems}

Two premalignant systems anchor this work. The primary system is the lung adenocarcinoma precursor
sequence, for which matched single-nucleus, spatial, and exome profiling of AAH, AIS, and invasive
lesions is now available (peng-luad; kadara-field). This cohort is unusually well suited to the
question because it spans an ordered, histologically defined progression series with paired molecular
and spatial data on the same specimens. Prior work in this system has implicated proinflammatory niches
and progenitor-like epithelial states in early progression (peng-luad), providing biological anchors
against which model-derived findings can be checked.

The secondary system is the pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) sequence, a graded
low-to-high-grade precursor series in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (panin-review). PanIN serves
here as a cross-disease transferability and calibration probe rather than a second discovery cohort:
it tests whether a method developed on lung precursors behaves sensibly when applied to a different
tissue with a much smaller number of profiled donors.

\section{Single-cell and spatial profiling of premalignant tissue}
\label{sec:intro-profiling}

Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing resolve the epithelial and microenvironmental cell
states present in premalignant tissue but discard spatial organization in the process of dissociation
(scrnaseq-review). Spatial transcriptomics, and in particular spot-based platforms such as 10x Visium,
restore tissue coordinates but at a resolution coarser than a single cell, so that each measured spot
mixes several cells (visium). Neither modality alone provides what a study of niche effects requires:
epithelial regulatory state, the composition of the surrounding niche, and the spatial relationship
between them. Integrating them---deconvolving spatial spots against a single-cell reference to recover
cell-type composition and decoded expression---is therefore a necessary preprocessing step
(destvi; deconvolution-review), and one whose adequacy must be checked rather than assumed.

A further difficulty is intrinsic to the data rather than the assay. Premalignant cohorts are
cross-sectional: each specimen is a snapshot of one stage, not a time course of one cell. Any account
of progression from such data is therefore an inference about the direction of state change aligned
with pathological stage, not an observation of a trajectory, and the analysis must be explicit about
that distinction (pseudotime-caveats).

\section{Optimal transport and neural differential equations for cell-state dynamics}
\label{sec:intro-ot}

Recovering direction-of-change from unpaired cross-sectional populations is the problem optimal
transport (OT) was built for: given cells at a source stage and cells at a target stage, OT constructs
the most economical coupling between them and thereby a map of how the population moves (ot-singlecell).
Entropic-regularized OT makes this computationally tractable at cohort scale through the Sinkhorn
iteration (cuturi-sinkhorn), and conditional formulations of OT allow the coupling to be constrained
to comparable subpopulations rather than matching arbitrary cells (bunne-cot; manupriya-cot). In
parallel, neural ordinary differential equations and flow-matching methods learn continuous velocity
fields that transport one distribution into another (neural-ode; flow-matching), and optimal-transport
conditional flow matching combines the two, learning a field whose trajectories respect an OT coupling
\autocite{Lipman2022-ns}. These tools make it possible to model progression as a learned dynamical flow through a
molecular state space rather than as a static comparison of stages.

\section{Spatially resolved and niche-aware methods}
\label{sec:intro-spatial}

A growing family of methods incorporates spatial context into single-cell analysis: neighborhood-based
representations that summarize each cell's surroundings \autocite{Singhal2024-rv}, niche and cellular-community detection
(niche-methods), and cell--cell communication inference from ligand--receptor co-expression
(liana; cellcell-review). These methods characterize where cell states co-occur and which signaling
channels are available, but they generally describe association rather than isolate a
context-attributable component of a dynamical process, and they rarely test whether the spatial
information they use is resolved at the scale they assume. The gap this dissertation addresses lies
precisely there---between describing spatial context and quantifying its specific, scale-resolved
contribution to progression.

\section{Structured and universal differential equations}
\label{sec:intro-ude}

Purely neural dynamical models are flexible but opaque: a black-box velocity field can reconstruct a
transition without exposing which biological programs drive it. Universal differential equations (UDEs)
address this by combining mechanistic, interpretable structure with neural residual terms, so that a
model can be both expressive and readable (ude-rackauckas). For a study whose goal is to attribute
progression to specific regulatory programs, this structure is essential: the interpretable terms carry
the biological claim, and the residuals absorb what the structure cannot express, with a diagnostic to
report how much of each is doing the work. Casting regulatory state in terms of curated pathway and
transcription-factor activities (progeny; collectri; decoupler) gives the structured terms a biological
vocabulary, so that a fitted field can be read as a statement about named programs rather than abstract
latent dimensions.

\section{The unresolved estimand, and the motivation from a prior formulation}
\label{sec:intro-estimand}

Bringing these threads together exposes the estimand that this dissertation targets and that prior
approaches leave implicit. An initial formulation of the StageBridge approach established that
premalignant progression is learnable: a niche-conditioned transition model, in which each epithelial
receiver's atlas-derived state and a receiver-centered set encoding of its spatial neighborhood were
combined and transported between adjacent stages by optimal-transport conditional flow matching,
reconstructed progression-associated transitions, and reduced variants that removed the niche or the
gating that mixed state and context reconstructed them less well---evidence that context carried useful
predictive information. That formulation, however, combined epithelial state and niche context within a
single gated predictor and operated in a broad latent state space. It therefore could not answer the
question that actually matters biologically: what portion of the prediction is specifically attributable
to context rather than to the cell's own state, and whether the useful context is resolved at the scale
of the individual cell's neighborhood or only at the coarser scale of the specimen. Predictive accuracy
and causal-style attribution are different targets, and a model optimized for the former does not
deliver the latter.

The unresolved quantity is therefore a \emph{conditional context residual}: the additional
progression-aligned regulatory displacement of an epithelial cell that is attributable to its local
tissue context, after accounting for the displacement already predicted by its intrinsic state, and
estimated only among ecologically comparable cells so that the comparison is not confounded by state.
Defining this quantity, establishing when it can be identified from cross-sectional spatial data, and
measuring it in real premalignant cohorts is the problem this dissertation sets out to solve.

\section{Objective and hypotheses}
\label{sec:intro-objective}

The objective of this dissertation is to define, validate, and apply a conditional context-residual
estimand for premalignant epithelial progression---to determine whether local tissue context alters
epithelial regulatory transport beyond intrinsic state, through which regulatory programs, and at what
spatial scale. The methodological realization of this objective is StageBridge, a structured universal
differential equation that decomposes progression velocity into an intrinsic field and a niche-gated
context field, with the context residual $R_{\text{cond}}$ as its primary estimand; its full
specification is given in Chapter~\ref{chap:methods}. Where the prior formulation combined state and
context in a single predictor, this decomposition makes their separation explicit; and the validation
here is adversarial rather than confirmatory: the transport coupling that defines the estimand is
selected against a synthetic benchmark with known ground truth, and an initial state-neutral coupling
that failed that benchmark was discarded before any real data were analyzed.

Three pre-specified, falsifiable hypotheses organize the analysis, stated formally in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} (Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}) and tested in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. The
\emph{central hypothesis} is that local cell-state context explains a reproducible component of
regulatory transport beyond intrinsic state---that a non-zero, patient-robust context residual exists
for some regulatory programs. The \emph{local-context hypothesis} asks the stronger question of whether
that contextual information is genuinely receiver-local, outperforming a coarser specimen-level ecology
and a matched shuffle. The \emph{cross-disease hypothesis} asks
whether the estimator behaves correctly under transfer to a much smaller pancreatic precursor
cohort---returning patient-robust programs only where the data support them, and a calibrated null
otherwise---rather than requiring that specific lung programs recur.

Chapter~\ref{chap:methods} develops the model, estimand, coupling, and identifiability benchmark;
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} tests the three hypotheses; and Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} interprets
them and their limits. The intended contribution is neither an unqualified positive effect nor a bare
negative, but a prespecified context-residual estimand together with a characterized recovery
boundary---a statement of what local tissue context contributes to premalignant progression, through
which programs, and at the spatial scale the available data can and cannot resolve.
